Prodigy defines productive dialogue as communication that is clear, responsive, respectful, and intellectually responsible. It includes 15 features organized into four dimensions. When evaluating a student’s conversation, base feedback only on observable conversational behavior: what the student said, asked, explained, acknowledged, or failed to address.
A) Content. This dimension concerns how well the student presents the topic in a clear, informative, reasoned, and credible way.
The features included in this dimension are:
•	a-1: Reasoning: The student explains why something is true, how something works, or how one idea leads to another. This includes explaining the importance or implications of their research.
•	a-2: Explaining: The student helps the layperson understand by using examples, analogies, metaphors or concrete details. This focuses on making an idea understandable, not necessarily on justifying it.
•	a-3: Clarity: Avoiding jargon, technical terms, acronyms, and overly complex phrasing.
•	a-4: Tailoring: Adapting the message to the characteristics of the layperson. For instance, if the layperson says she is interested in sports, and then the student tries to give an explanation taken from the field of sports – it is tailoring.
•	a-5: Credibility: Referring to reliable knowledge sources. For instance, if the student says something like: “A research conducted at Harvard shows that…”. Avoid rewarding vague appeals to authority without substance.
B) Interpersonal rapport. This dimension concerns how the student builds a respectful, warm, and constructive relationship with the layperson.
•	b-1: Stressing similarities: The student highlights shared experiences, values, interests, or concerns that create common ground with the layperson.
•	b-2: Empathy and Benevolence: Being empathic and polite, avoiding accusatory, competitive or prescriptive language, conveying kindness, goodwill, and concern.
For example, if a student says: "I completely understand why this topic might worry you”, or “It was really nice talking to you”, or “Thank you for taking interest in my research”.
•	b-3: Respect: The student explicitly treats the layperson’s questions, ideas, concerns, or contributions as valuable. For example, they acknowledge a question as important or meaningful rather than merely answering it.
•	b-4: Sharing personal details: Sharing personal experiences or anecdotes. Especially when the student tells about their personal background, what excites or frustrates them about their research, what brought them to take interest in this field to begin with, and so on.
C) Perspective-taking and listening. This dimension concerns whether the student actively listens, invites participation, and responds to the layperson’s perspective.
•	c-1: Paraphrasing: If the student repeats what the layperson said in their own words, to confirm understanding. For instance, if the student says: “So, what you’re saying is that…” and the paraphrases what the layperson said, or: “If I understand correctly, you are concerned that…” and then restates what the layperson is concerned about.
•	c-2: Invitations to share: The student asks open-ended questions that invite the layperson to share their thoughts, experiences, knowledge, interests, or concerns.
•	c-3: Building on the partner’s ideas: The student takes something the layperson said and meaningfully extends, connects, develops, or integrates it into the conversation.
D) Integrity and Humility. This dimension emphasises ethical and responsible dialogue participation. The features included in this dimension are:
•	d-1: Intellectual humility: The student acknowledges limits in their own knowledge, expertise, role, or ability to answer. This refers to the speaker’s personal limitations of knowledge and expertise.
•	d-2: Transparency: Disclosing one’s position and values towards the subject/topic of conversation. For example, during a discussion about the use of nuclear energy, the speaker said: “I should share that I’m personally in favor of nuclear energy as a solution to our energy crisis.”
•	d-3: Tolerating uncertainty: The student acknowledges uncertainty, complexity, limitations of current evidence, open questions, or constraints in the field. This refers to uncertainty in the science or situation, not merely the student’s own lack of knowledge.
